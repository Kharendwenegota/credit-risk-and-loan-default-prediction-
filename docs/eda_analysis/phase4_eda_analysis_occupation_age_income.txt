EDA ANALYSIS: DEFAULT RATE BY OCCUPATION, AGE, AND INCOME
Credit Risk & Loan Default Prediction Project — Phase 4

Charts covered in this analysis:
    1. Default Rate by Occupation
    2. Default Rate by Age Group
    3. Default Rate by Income Band (see also: income_band_risk_analysis.txt
       for the full investigative deep-dive on this chart)


===========================================================
1. DEFAULT RATE BY OCCUPATION
===========================================================

OBSERVATION
-----------
Highest default rates:
    1. Low-skill Laborers        (highest)
    2. Drivers
    3. Waiters/Barmen staff

Lowest default rates:
    - Accountants
    - High-skill Tech staff

INTERPRETATION
--------------
This pattern lines up with income stability and job security rather
than income level alone. Low-skill labor, driving, and service
occupations (waitstaff) tend to have:
    - More variable or seasonal income
    - Less job security (easier to lose employment, fewer contractual
      protections)
    - Typically lower financial cushion / savings buffer

Accountants and high-skill tech staff, by contrast, tend to have:
    - More stable, salaried employment
    - Higher and more predictable income
    - Greater financial resilience to short-term shocks

BUSINESS IMPLICATION
---------------------
Occupation type is a meaningful, low-cost risk signal available at
the point of application, before any credit history is even
considered. It should be retained as a modeling feature, and can
support tiered underwriting policies (e.g. differing affordability
buffers by occupation risk tier) at the business level.

CAVEAT
------
Occupation type may correlate with (and partially proxy for) income
level and employment type, both of which are separately captured
elsewhere in the model. Feature importance / SHAP analysis in
Phase 6 should be used to confirm occupation still contributes
independent predictive value once these other features are included,
rather than assuming this bivariate pattern holds in the full model.


===========================================================
2. DEFAULT RATE BY AGE GROUP
===========================================================

OBSERVATION
-----------
Default rate declines steadily and consistently as age increases.
Applicants in their 20s show the highest default rates; each
subsequent age band shows a lower rate, continuing through the oldest
age group observed (60-70).

INTERPRETATION
--------------
This is a genuine, monotonic risk gradient rather than a flat
baseline risk across ages. Plausible drivers include:
    - Younger applicants are earlier in their careers, with less
      established income and employment history
    - Younger applicants have had less time to build credit history
      or savings buffers
    - Financial habits and obligations (e.g. dependents, established
      housing costs) stabilize with age

This should NOT be read as "default risk exists equally across all
ages" — the data shows a clear, actionable gradient, not a constant
baseline.

BUSINESS IMPLICATION
---------------------
Age (or a proxy such as years of employment / EMPLOYMENT_TO_AGE_RATIO,
already engineered in Phase 3) is a strong, simple risk signal.
Younger applicants may warrant additional affordability checks,
smaller initial credit limits, or closer alignment between loan size
and demonstrated income stability, rather than blanket restriction.

CAVEAT
------
Age-based lending decisions can raise fair-lending / discrimination
concerns depending on jurisdiction and regulatory context (relevant
given this project's South African NCA framing). Age should inform
model features and risk-based pricing, not blanket eligibility
rules, and any operational use should be reviewed against applicable
consumer credit regulation.


===========================================================
3. DEFAULT RATE BY INCOME BAND
===========================================================

OBSERVATION
-----------
Initial pattern appeared non-monotonic: "Medium" income band showed a
higher default rate than "Low" income, which was unexpected.

INTERPRETATION (full investigation in income_band_risk_analysis.txt)
--------------
Deeper investigation showed this was NOT a genuine effect of income
level. The "Very Low" income band contains a disproportionately high
share of pensioners (31.4% vs. 9.6-18.4% in other bands), who have
low but highly stable, guaranteed income. This artificially lowers
the Very Low band's observed default rate and masks the true
underlying pattern.

Once this compositional effect is accounted for (via
ANNUITY_TO_INCOME_RATIO, which reflects actual affordability burden),
the real pattern is a clean, monotonic decline in risk as the share
of income committed to loan repayment decreases — from 24.0% of
income at the lowest tier down to 12.4% at the highest.

BUSINESS IMPLICATION
---------------------
Income LEVEL alone is an incomplete and potentially misleading risk
signal. Income STABILITY / TYPE (e.g. pensioner vs. working-age,
salaried vs. commercial/self-employed) and affordability ratios
(payment burden relative to income) are more reliable predictors of
default than raw income level.

RECOMMENDATION
--------------
Any dashboard or model built on this data should segment or control
for income type when presenting income-based risk findings, to avoid
presenting a misleading or confusing headline pattern.


===========================================================
CROSS-CUTTING TAKEAWAY FOR THE PROJECT
===========================================================
Across all three charts, a consistent theme emerges: STABILITY
(of income, employment, and life stage) is a stronger and more
consistent driver of default risk than raw LEVEL (of income,
job prestige, or age alone). This theme should carry through into
the dashboard narrative, the feature engineering already completed
in Phase 3 (e.g. EMPLOYMENT_TO_AGE_RATIO, ANNUITY_TO_INCOME_RATIO,
NO_BUREAU_HISTORY flags), and the eventual model interpretation in
Phase 6 (SHAP analysis) — where stability-related features should be
expected to carry meaningful predictive weight.
